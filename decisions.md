## 2026-11-18: The decision is the most I should offer for a house near Reston

- Decision. I study one choice: for a listed house within about 20 miles of Reston, Virginia, what is the most I should offer? I estimate the sale price in dollars with a low, middle, and high estimate. My offer is the low estimate at my comfort level. I compare my model with the rule buyers use, the zip code's median price per square foot times square feet, by how many held-out houses each would have won at the same comfort level without overpaying more often.
- Why. I am house hunting now, so I can judge the results myself. Fairfax County publishes individual sales with property details. A test query on 2026-11-18 found about 96,000 sales since January 2020 labeled "Valid and verified sale", with the latest in September 2026. Overpaying is the costlier mistake for me. A 10% comfort level means a dollar overpaid counts 9 times as much as a dollar offered too low (0.90 divided by 0.10 is 9).
- Alternative. Why this, not that. I rejected predicting aircraft icing at altitude from pilot reports and weather balloons. I am house hunting now, so I can judge the house results myself. I could not check icing results against my own experience.
- Revisit. I would change the question if the county data cannot be joined into enough clean sales with square feet, year built, and zip code to fit and test a model. I would first add nearby counties before changing the decision itself.

## 2026-11-18: Data source is Fairfax County public records, Fairfax only

- Decision. I use the Fairfax County open data tables from the Department of Tax Administration: sales, dwelling details, address and zip, land use, lot size, and assessed values. They join by parcel ID. I downloaded sales from January 2015 onward on 2026-11-18, with every county sale label kept. I do not republish the files. `data/README.md` gives the download command and a SHA-256 for each file.
- Why. Fairfax contains Reston. The download has 350,708 recorded transfers since 2015, and 173,472 of them carry the county label "Valid and verified sale". The county disclaimer reserves all rights and allows copies for internal or personal use, so I do not create a `data` release.
- Alternative. Why this, not that. I rejected adding Loudoun County now. I found parcel layers for Loudoun but no county download with sale prices, and two counties record house details differently. I also rejected paid data aggregators, because I could not share how to rebuild the data.
- Revisit. I would add Loudoun if I find a public source with sale prices and house details. Until then the model covers the Fairfax part of my 20 miles, not Sterling or Ashburn.

## 2026-11-18: Which sales I keep

- Decision. I keep sales from January 2020 to September 2026, single family houses and townhouses only, with the county label "Valid and verified sale", and a price from $50,000 to $5,000,000. I drop sales with no living area, year built, bedrooms, or zip code, and I treat the placeholder years 1700, 1730, and 2100 as missing. I keep one copy of exact duplicate rows. I drop sales dated before the year built or before the recorded remodel year, and I keep sales in the same year. The baseline is a common rule of thumb, the zip code's median price per square foot times square feet, and it uses the same sales.
- Why. Since 2020 there are 44,009 valid single family sales and 27,751 valid townhouse sales, enough to fit and test a model. The county label is the county's own definition of a market sale, so I do not have to invent one. Of all valid home sales since 2015, 4 are under $50,000 (three at $0, one at $7,000) and 126 are over $5,000,000. A $0 price has no logarithm, and I am not shopping for a $5,000,000 house. The limits are round numbers I fixed before fitting any model. The county describes each house as it is today, so a sale before the year built or the remodel year has the wrong house attached: 487 and 683 valid sales since 2020.
- Alternative. Why this, not that. (1) I rejected keeping 2015 to 2019. Prices then are far from today's, and older sales are more likely to have a house that changed since. I can still train on fewer years in the model step, but not on more. (2) I rejected condos. Their price depends on monthly fees, floor, and building, which the data does not have, and 37,271 of 37,314 valid condo sales have no lot size. (3) I rejected adding "Handyman special" sales (3,963). They are real prices for houses in poor condition, but a listing does not come with that label, so I could not use it for a house I am looking at. (4) I rejected dropping same-year remodel sales (3,410). Many are likely houses renovated and then sold, which the county describes correctly.
- Revisit. I would widen the years if the model needs more sales per zip code. I would add condos if I start shopping for one. I would rethink the remodel rule if the residuals show that same-year remodel sales are priced very differently.

## 2026-11-18: Two more cleaning rules for recording errors

- Decision. I drop sales with a living area under 400 square feet (2 rows). I drop both rows when the same house has two different prices on the same day (3 pairs, 6 rows). This leaves 70,306 sales: single family houses and townhouses in Fairfax County, January 2020 to September 2026.
- Why. One house was recorded at 4 square feet and sold for $1,700,100, which is $425,025 per square foot. That is a recording error, and it would distort the median price per square foot of its zip code. For the same-day pairs, for example $632,500 and $600,000 for one Reston house on 2022-03-28, I cannot tell which price is real.
- Alternative. Why this, not that. I rejected keeping these rows, because the baseline is built from price per square foot and one wrong area can move a zip code's median. For the pairs, I rejected keeping the higher or the lower price, because I have no reason to prefer one.
- Revisit. I would change the 400 square feet limit if I find real small houses being dropped. Only one such sale exists now, at 336 square feet.

## 2026-11-18: Split by time, cutoff 2026-03-01

- Decision. Sales before 2026-03-01 are training data: 64,490 sales from 2020-01-02 to 2026-02-28. Sales from 2026-03-01 to 2026-09-15 are test data: 5,816 sales. The split is by date, so there is no random seed. Every number the baseline or the model uses comes from the training sales. The test sales are scored once, at the end. I fit the regression with statsmodels.
- Why. I will use the model on houses that sell after my data ends, so the test has to be later than the training data. Six and a half months of test sales let me see whether the error grows as the sale date moves further from the training data. September 2026 is a partial month with 228 sales, because the county data stops on 2026-09-15. statsmodels prints each feature's effect with its uncertainty, and it also has quantile regression.
- Alternative. Why this, not that. I rejected a random split. Prices drift over time, and a random split would let the model see sales from the same months it is tested on. I rejected testing on the last 12 months, because the newest training sale would be a year older than the latest test sale, which is harder than my real use. I rejected testing on June to September 2026 only, about 3,070 sales, because fewer test sales give a wider uncertainty range on the share of houses won. I rejected scikit-learn because it does not report the uncertainty of each effect.
- Revisit. I would move the cutoff if the county publishes newer sales before December 9. I would then keep the same rule: the test is the most recent months.

## 2026-11-18: How I score offers (corrects the first entry)

- Decision. For each offer on a held-out sale I record two things. Overpaid: my offer is above the sale price. Close: my offer is between 95% and 100% of the sale price. I report the share overpaid, the average dollars overpaid, and the share of close offers, for the model and for the rule of thumb at the same comfort level. This replaces "houses won" from my first entry. I changed the proposal to match.
- Why. In my first entry, "won" meant an offer at or above the sale price. That is the same event as overpaying, so the two shares were always equal. Also, any method that is honest about a 10% comfort level overpays about 10% of the time, so that share cannot separate two methods. On a practice test inside the training data (3,439 sales from September 2025 to February 2026), both methods overpaid on about 10%. But the model's overpaid offers were over by $72,002 on average and the rule of thumb's by $137,040, and the model made 425 close offers against 212.
- Alternative. Why this, not that. I rejected keeping "houses won", because it repeats the overpaid column. I rejected using only the typical offer as a percent of the sale price (88% against 81%), because a count of close offers is easier to picture. The 95% line is my own choice. A real seller may accept less or want more.
- Revisit. I would change the 95% line if an agent tells me a different gap is normal for accepted offers in this area.

## 2026-11-18: The baseline uses the last 12 months of training sales

- Decision. The rule of thumb is the zip code's median price per square foot times the house's square feet. The median uses training sales from the last 12 months before the cutoff, 2025-03-01 to 2026-02-28, with houses and townhouses together. A zip code with fewer than 30 sales in that window uses the county-wide median.
- Why. Median price per square foot rose about 35% from early 2020 to late 2025. On the practice test, a median over all training years was about 13% too low, and its typical miss was 14.8%. A median from the last 12 months had a typical miss of 11.7%. I want the baseline to be a fair opponent, not one weakened by old prices.
- Alternative. Why this, not that. I rejected all training years because of the drift. I rejected separate medians for houses and townhouses (typical miss 11.0%), because that is no longer the plain rule of thumb that listing sites show.
- Revisit. I would use a shorter window if prices start moving fast again, as they did in 2021.

## 2026-11-18: The model is a linear regression on log price, trained on the last 12 months

- Decision. I fit a linear regression on the log of the sale price with statsmodels. The features are the log of living area, property type, zip code, age at sale, bedrooms, full baths, half baths, and the log of lot size. I train on the same 12 months as the baseline, 2025-03-01 to 2026-02-28 (8,458 sales), with no term for the sale date. A house with no recorded lot size gets a "lot missing" marker. An empty half bath field counts as zero. Zip codes with fewer than 30 training sales are pooled into one group. There is no random seed, because nothing is drawn at random.
- Why. Log price: in raw dollars the error spread is about $140,000 for the smallest fifth of houses and about $460,000 for the largest fifth. On the log scale it stays between 0.21 and 0.28, so one spread can serve all houses. Features: on the practice test the typical miss fell from 12.0% (living area and type) to 8.3% with zip code, 7.3% with age, bedrooms, and baths, and 6.4% with lot size. Window: training on the last 12 months with no time term had a typical miss of 6.6%, and its 10% offers were above the sale price 9.9% of the time.
- Alternative. Why this, not that. (1) I rejected the county's grade and condition ratings, although they lowered the typical miss to 5.7%. They describe the house today, and an assessor can change them after a sale, so they may contain the sale price. (2) I rejected a straight-line time trend. It kept rising while prices went flat, and its 10% offers overpaid 26% of the time. (3) I rejected training on 24 months with no time term. Prices rose inside that window, and its 10% offers overpaid only 6.5% of the time, so it was more cautious than it claimed. (4) I rejected one price level per quarter over all years (typical miss 6.7%). It was as good, but harder to explain, and the newest quarter would have only two months of sales. (5) I rejected raw dollars for the reason above.
- Revisit. Training on 12 months assumes prices are flat inside the window. That was true for the last year and false in 2021. If prices move fast again, I would add a time term or shorten the window.

## 2026-11-18: Low and high estimates, and a 10% comfort level

- Decision. For each training sale I compute the ratio of the real price to the method's fair price. I sort the ratios and read off cut points, separately for single family houses and townhouses. The low estimate at 10% is the fair price times the ratio that 10% of training sales fall below. My offer is the 10% low estimate. My comfort level is 10%: at most a 10% chance of paying more than the sale price. The rule of thumb gets its low estimates the same way, from its own ratios. I report 5% and 25% next to 10%.
- Why. The loss behind the rule: a dollar overpaid counts 9, a dollar offered too low counts 1, and losing a house has no extra cost. The offer that makes this loss smallest on average is the price the house falls below 10% of the time, because 1 divided by (1 plus 9) is 0.10. On the practice test at 10%, the model overpaid on 9.9% of sales and 12.4% of its offers were close. At 25% it overpaid on 26.8%, and at 5% only 5.6% of offers were close. Separate cut points by type: with one set, the model overpaid on 12.0% of single family houses and 6.5% of townhouses.
- Alternative. Why this, not that. (1) I rejected a cost for losing a house. My cost of losing is close to zero because there are other houses, and a rule built on it alone would say never buy. (2) I rejected a bell curve for the errors. Its 10% estimate sat above only 8% of training sales, because real errors have heavier tails. (3) I rejected quantile regression. Its overpaid offers were smaller ($45,852 against $65,241 in one comparison), but I could not explain it in a three-minute talk. (4) I rejected 25% and 5% for the numbers above.
- Revisit. On houses the model values above $1.5 million, its 10% offers overpaid 19.6% of the time on the practice test. If I shop in that range, I need a spread that grows with price, or quantile regression.
